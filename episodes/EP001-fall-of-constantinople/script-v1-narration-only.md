## Chapter 1 — Cold open

[ON SCREEN: tối đen; tiếng gió; chữ khắc dần "CONSTANTINOPLE." + dòng italic "Anno Domini MCDLIII"; bản đồ khắc thành phố, tường Theodosian]
L01
For more than a thousand years, nothing could break the walls of Constantinople. Arab armies tried, twice. Fleets tried. Even a Crusader army managed to get in, in twelve oh four, though that one hardly counts, since it was supposedly on the city's side. Then, in the spring of fourteen fifty-three, a twenty-one-year-old sultan parked his army outside them. In just under eight weeks, the Roman Empire was over.
<!-- B#1-3, WHE 674–678/717–718 sieges, 1204; B#4 -->

[ON SCREEN: ba ô khắc xếp cạnh nhau, mỗi ô một nghi phạm: khẩu pháo · cánh cửa nhỏ · người đàn ông bị thương; nhãn FIG. I / II / III]
L02
Here's the thing. Ask historians what actually broke those walls, and you won't get an answer. You'll get an argument. Some will blame a cannon. Some will blame a small door that somebody forgot to lock. And some will blame one wounded man, carried off the wall at exactly the wrong moment.

L03
Three suspects. One city. And a set of witnesses that includes a Venetian who disliked the Genoese, a Genoese archbishop who disliked the Venetians, and a Greek chronicler with firm opinions about almost everyone. So we'll go carefully. By the end, I'll tell you which suspect I would arrest. Or, and this is the uncomfortable possibility, why the question itself may be slightly wrong.
<!-- B#21, #24; Barbaro/Leonard/Ducas hostile (Pears) -->

[BEAT 1.2 s]
[ON SCREEN: loupe hạ xuống → archive light table: tư liệu thật — bản đồ/ảnh bản thảo cuộc vây (cần tìm); nhãn "28 MAY 1453 · EVENING"]
L04
But first, the scene. It is the evening of the twenty-eighth of May, fourteen fifty-three. The emperor is riding to Hagia Sophia, the Great Church, to make his peace with God. Beyond the walls, the Ottoman camp glows like a second city. Inside, fewer than seven thousand men are waiting for something like eighty thousand to come at them.
<!-- Runciman leaf 159 (emperor to cathedral), 110 (<7,000 vs ~80,000) -->

L05
Hold that picture. We'll come back to it. But to understand how it came to this, we need to meet the man on that road, and the empire he inherited, which by then fit comfortably inside a single set of walls.

## Chapter 2 — The last Roman

[ON SCREEN: chân dung/khắc hoàng đế (không có chân dung đương thời đáng tin — dùng huy hiệu Palaiologos/ đại bàng hai đầu, xác minh nguồn PD); chữ "CONSTANTINE XI · 1404–1453"]
L06
His name was Constantine Palaiologos. Constantine the Eleventh, if you're counting, and the Byzantines were. He was born in fourteen oh four, the fourth son of an emperor, which is not a position that comes with high expectations. His brother John, the emperor, died without an heir in October fourteen forty-eight. In January fourteen forty-nine, Constantine was proclaimed emperor in a small ceremony in the Peloponnese. He was never crowned in Hagia Sophia, as custom required. Which tells you roughly how the empire was doing.
<!-- B#7; Wikipedia Constantine XI -->

[ON SCREEN: bản đồ khắc: đế quốc 1450 thu lại thành chấm; thang "Rome → one city"]
L07
And what, exactly, was he emperor of? By fourteen-fifty, the Romans, and yes, they still called themselves Romans, held one city, a few islands, and the southern Peloponnese. The great capital had been captured once before, in twelve oh four, by Crusaders, and it had never really recovered. Constantine had no children, and every attempt to find a wife, in Aragon, in Georgia, in Trebizond, fell through. An emperor with no heir, no wife, and not much of an empire. But he did have the walls.
<!-- B#2, #3, #8 -->

[ON SCREEN: mặt cắt khắc tường: hào 60 ft → tường ngoài 25 ft → tường trong 40 ft + tháp; nhãn nhỏ "MESOTEICHION" chỉ vào thung lũng Lycus]
L08
So let's talk about those walls, because everything that follows hangs on them. On the land side, they ran for about four miles, and they came in layers. First a ditch, around sixty feet wide. Then a low outer wall, some twenty-five feet high. Then, behind it, a taller inner wall, forty feet or more, studded with towers. An attacker had to cross all of it, under fire. They had been standing since the fifth century. There was only one soft spot, a dip in the ground where a little river valley, the Lycus, cut through the line. The Greeks called it the Mesoteichion, the middle wall. Remember that name.
<!-- Pears pp.237–242 (Dethier, Van Millingen); Runciman leaf 112 (Theodosius II; triple wall; foss ~60 ft) -->

[ON SCREEN: tranh/ bản thảo thế kỷ 15 về Hagia Sophia hoặc cảnh nhà thờ (cần tìm PD); chữ "12 DECEMBER 1452"]
L09
Now, the walls were one problem. The other was the Pope. Rome would send help, but only if the Greek Church formally accepted union with the Catholic Church. On the twelfth of December, fourteen fifty-two, that union was proclaimed in Hagia Sophia itself. A great many people hated it. A monk named Gennadius warned that the city would be lost and its people sold into slavery, and a riot followed. One chronicler says plenty of people accepted the union only because they planned to quietly undo it once the Turks went home. Nothing brings a besieged city together like a theological argument, in the sense that everyone is furious at the same time.
<!-- B#9; Pears pp.203–205 (Ducas, Gennadius) -->

L10
So that's the defender: a thin empire, a divided church, a very good wall, and almost nobody to stand on it. Now meet the man who was coming to knock, and the most expensive job application in medieval history, which was sent to the wrong address.

## Chapter 3 — The boy sultan

[ON SCREEN: Gentile Bellini, chân dung Mehmed II (1480, PD — kiểm Commons); khắc lại thành roundel FIG. IV]
L11
Mehmed the Second was born on the thirtieth of March, fourteen thirty-two, the son of a sultan and a mother who had been a slave. He was handed the throne at twelve, when his father, Murad, stepped down. It lasted about two years. Hungary broke a truce, the advisers panicked, and Murad came back to lead the army. The boy sultan was sent off to govern a province instead. Imagine being a sultan at twelve and being retired by fourteen. It does something to a person. In Mehmed's case, it seems to have produced a very particular kind of determination.
<!-- B#4; Wikipedia Mehmed II (İnalcık: recall pushed mainly by Halil Pasha) -->

L12
His father died in February fourteen fifty-one, and Mehmed took the throne again, at nineteen. Later writers say that one of his first acts was to have his infant half-brother drowned. The historian Edwin Pears warns that both Turkish and Christian writers are unreliable on Mehmed, so take that detail with a pinch of salt. What isn't in doubt is the voice at his elbow. A scholar named Akshamsaddin had been telling him since boyhood that Constantinople was his to take.
<!-- Pears pp.206–211 (doubt); Wikipedia Mehmed II -->

L12b
The emperor, for his part, made an early and expensive mistake. Back in fourteen fifty-one, the Ottomans were paying the Byzantines to keep a rival claimant to the throne, a prince named Orhan, safely in Constantinople. Constantine's envoys now asked for the allowance to be doubled, and hinted that Orhan might otherwise be let go. It was a bluff. Mehmed didn't blink, and he now had a perfect excuse for war. Two years later, Orhan tried to escape the fallen city dressed as a Greek monk. Another prisoner betrayed him, and he was beheaded on the spot.
<!-- Pears pp.213–217 (envoys, Orhan pension); Wikipedia Constantine XI (1451 threat backfired); Runciman leaf 177 (Orhan disguised as monk, betrayed, decapitated) -->

[ON SCREEN: ảnh/ khắc Rumeli Hisarı trên eo biển; thước đo "660 m"; thanh timeline: Apr–Aug 1452]
L13
In the spring of fourteen fifty-two, he showed everyone what he meant. At the narrowest point of the Bosphorus, about six hundred and sixty meters across, he began to build a fortress. Right on the European shore, which was Byzantine territory, in defiance of the treaties. He supervised the work himself. The emperor's envoys protested; Mehmed fobbed them off. By the end of August it was finished, and the Turks gave it a name: Boghaz-kesen. The Cutter of the Strait. Or, depending on how you translate it, the Cutter of the Throat. Subtle, he was not.
<!-- B#5; Runciman leaf 89 (31 Aug 1452; Boghaz-kesen); Pears pp.213–217 (envoys) -->

L14
That November, a Venetian ship ignored the signal to stop. The fortress's guns brought it down. The captain, a man named Rizzo, was impaled and left by the roadside, and his crew were beheaded. Until then, many in the West had hoped the young sultan was bluffing. That was the end of the hope.
<!-- B#6; Runciman leaf 90 (Rizzo) -->

## Chapter 4 — The gunfounder

L15
Fortresses and fleets are one thing. Walls like Constantinople's are another. For those, Mehmed needed something bigger, and here's the irony: he got it from the emperor's own doorstep. Sometime around fourteen fifty-two, a cannon-founder named Urban turned up in Constantinople. Hungarian, say some sources. Wallachian, say others. Everyone agrees he was excellent at casting bronze guns, and rather unlucky at being paid.
<!-- Pears pp.231–232; Runciman leaf 101 -->

[ON SCREEN: khắc "bombard" lớn trên xe kéo; nhãn italic "the great bombard"; số leader "60 oxen · 200 men"]
L16
He offered his services to the emperor. Constantine could not afford his salary, and could not even supply the metal. So Urban left, and went to see the sultan. Mehmed questioned him closely. Could he make a gun that would break the walls of Constantinople? Urban said he could make one that would blast the walls of Babylon. He was given four times the salary he'd asked of the emperor. Which is a lesson in recruitment, if nothing else.
<!-- Runciman leaf 101–103; Pears (Ducas: would have accepted a quarter) -->

L17
Now, to be fair, Mehmed had plenty of other cannon. But the monster, the one the chroniclers kept coming back to, began life as an unpaid bill. It was hauled toward the city that winter by sixty oxen, with two hundred men walking alongside to steady it and another two hundred ahead, preparing the road. By the end of March, it was within five miles of the walls.
<!-- Pears pp.231–232 (Ducas) -->

L18
How big was it? Honestly, nobody agrees. The sources give a stone ball weighing hundreds of kilos, somewhere between roughly two hundred and seventy and five hundred and fifty, depending on whom you trust. What they do agree on is how often it could fire. Seven times a day. That's all. The gun needed so much tending that it could barely manage a handful of shots before the sun went down. Seven shots a day. Remember that number. It matters later.
<!-- B#16 (D8), Runciman leaf 120, Pears pp.245–246 -->

[BEAT 1.4 s]
L19
Meanwhile, inside the city, the emperor wanted to know exactly how many men he had. He asked his adviser, George Sphrantzes, to count every man who could carry a weapon. Sphrantzes did the sums and brought back the total. The emperor, we're told, wanted it kept secret. In a moment, you'll see why.
<!-- Pears pp.247–248 (census "told to keep secret"); Wikipedia Constantine XI (Sphrantzes adviser) -->

## Chapter 5 — Fifty-three days

[ON SCREEN: số khắc lớn "4,983" + "< 2,000" → tổng "< 7,000"; dưới là dải "≈ 80,000"; chú thích "14 miles of walls"]
L20
The number was four thousand, nine hundred and eighty-three. That is how many Greek men, in all of Constantinople, could be found who were fit to fight. Add fewer than two thousand foreigners, Genoese, Venetians and others, and you get under seven thousand men for fourteen miles of walls. Against them, about eighty thousand soldiers, plus a mass of irregulars. The Venetian Barbaro wrote down a hundred and fifty thousand. The Greeks, doing the arithmetic in their heads, said three or four hundred thousand. Fear is a generous mathematician.
<!-- B#12–13, Runciman leaf 99, 110; Pears pp.222–223, 247–248 (D2, D10) -->

L21
Help of a kind had arrived in late January. A Genoese nobleman, Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, sailed in with about seven hundred armed men. A professional soldier, a siege specialist. The emperor put him in charge of the land walls. One story says he was promised the island of Lemnos if the city held. Whatever the terms, he took the worst job in the place: the Mesoteichion, the weak spot in the valley, which he held with something like two thousand men.
<!-- B#10, #11 (L/? — hedged "one story"); Pears pp.307–309 -->

[ON SCREEN: khắc pháo bắn vào tường ban ngày; ban đêm đội sửa tường mang gỗ/đá/thùng; nhãn "stone · timber · barrels · earth · hides"]
L22
On the sixth of April, the bombardment began, and it went on, with a kind of ceaseless monotony, for more than six weeks. The guns were clumsy. They slid off their platforms in the April mud. The big ones, Urban's included, could be fired just seven times a day. But each of those seven shots did enormous damage. Every night, the defenders came out and mended the wall with whatever they had: stones, timber, barrels, earth, hides. Every morning, the guns knocked it down again. A Venetian doctor named Nicolò Barbaro kept a diary of it all.
<!-- B#1, #17; Runciman leaf 120; Pears pp.309–310 (stockade); Hektoen (Barbaro) -->

[ON SCREEN: bản đồ khắc Golden Horn: xích từ Tháp Eugenius sang tháp Pera; mười thuyền; mũi tên đỏ từ hạm đội Ottoman]
L23
Then there was the harbor. A great chain stretched across the mouth of the Golden Horn, held up on wooden floats, from a tower on the city side to one on the walls of Genoese Pera. Ten ships guarded it. As long as that chain held, the city's long sea walls along the harbor were safe, and the sultan's fleet was kept out.
<!-- Runciman leaf 110, 117; Pears pp.222, 250 -->

[ON SCREEN: khắc trận hải chiến 20/4: bốn tàu cao buồm giữa nhiều thuyền nhỏ; nhãn "20 APRIL"; sultan trên bờ]
L24
On the twentieth of April, four ships, three Genoese and one loaded with grain for the emperor, came up from the south. The Ottoman fleet went at them. For hours it looked as though the four would be boarded and taken, with the sultan watching from the shore, shouting instructions about seamanship, a subject on which, we're told, he knew nothing at all. Then the wind, which had died, came back. The great sails filled, and the four ships crashed through the Turkish craft and into the harbor behind the chain. Mehmed's admiral, Baltoghlu, was stripped of his rank and beaten with sticks.
<!-- Pears pp.257–267; Runciman leaf 124–127 (bastinado) -->

L25
The careful chronicler Critobulus counts a little over a hundred Turks dead that day, and twenty-two Christians. The Archbishop of Mytilene, Leonard of Chios, says ten thousand Turks. History is full of men whose arithmetic improves with distance.
<!-- Pears pp.266–267 (Critobulus vs Leonard 10,000, Phrantzes 12,000) -->

[BEAT 1.4 s]
L26
That should have been the moment the siege turned. It wasn't, because two days later Mehmed did something that isn't supposed to be possible. He moved a navy over a hill.

[ON SCREEN: khắc/ bản đồ: Bosphorus → đồi sau Galata → Golden Horn; tuyến đứt nét đỏ; xe lăn gỗ; bò; ròng rọc; chữ "22 APRIL · FIRST LIGHT"]
L27
At first light on Sunday, the twenty-second of April, the defenders looked across the harbor and saw ships. Ottoman ships, inside the Golden Horn, behind the chain. Somewhere between seventy and eighty light vessels had been hauled overland, up over the hill behind Galata, on wooden cradles and greased tracks, pulled by men and oxen and pulleys, in a single night. All the while, the sultan's guns pounded the harbor so that the smoke and the noise would cover what was happening. One account says the idea came from an Italian in his service.
<!-- Pears pp.262ff; Runciman leaf 128 (probably an Italian; dawn Sunday 22 April; cannon behind Pera) -->

L28
Think about that for a moment. The sultan's biggest gun, designed by a Hungarian or Wallachian. His cleverest maneuver, perhaps suggested by an Italian. Christendom, it turns out, was perfectly happy to sell the sultan its ideas, so long as the invoice cleared. And now the defenders had to find more men for the harbor walls, from a pool of men that was already almost empty.

L29
They tried to strike back. On the twenty-eighth of April, a fire-raid on the Ottoman ships in the harbor went ahead, delayed at Genoese request, and by then the Turks were ready. A boat was sunk, and some eighty or ninety men were lost. Prisoners on both sides were executed in full view of the other. Accusations flew, Venetians against Genoese, Genoese against Venetians, and Pears, writing in nineteen oh three, concluded that the Genoese were guilty of selfishness, but not of treason.
<!-- Pears (Coco, 28 Apr; Genoese neutral "selfish and fatal" but not treacherous) -->

L30
Then came the tunnels. Ottoman miners dug under the walls at several points. Inside the city, an engineer named John Grant dug counter-tunnels, met them underground, and wrecked them. On the seventh of May, thirty thousand men attacked. On the twelfth, fifty thousand. Both were thrown back. A great wooden tower the Turks rolled up on the eighteenth was burned by the defenders with gunpowder. Each time, the city held. Each time, it had fewer men to hold with.
<!-- Pears (mining, John Grant; assaults 7 May ~30,000, 12 May ~50,000; tower 18 May) -->

[ON SCREEN: khắc thuyền brigantine nhỏ ra khơi; ngày "3 MAY" → "23 MAY"; câu italic trích Pears]
L31
Back on the third of May, a small ship had slipped out of the harbor to look for the Venetian relief fleet. On the twenty-third, it came back. It had found nothing. The crew could have sailed for home. They voted instead to return. Whether the city be taken or not, one sailor said, whether it was to life or to death, their duty was to go back. Hold on to that sentence. A great deal of this story is about people who stayed.
<!-- Pears pp.277–300 (brigantine; sailor's words, paraphrased PD) -->

## Chapter 6 — Omens

L32
And now, the city began to see signs. During a procession, an icon of the Virgin slipped and fell. A storm broke. On the night of the full moon, the moon went dark for three hours. Then a thick fog settled over Constantinople, and when it lifted, a strange light was seen shining on the dome of Hagia Sophia. Some took it as the Holy Spirit leaving. Some hoped it was the campfires of a Hungarian relief army. Pears, writing about it in nineteen oh three, says it's hard to say which parts of the story are true and which are myth. Some modern scholars suggest the red skies came from volcanic ash. Whatever it was, the city read it as an answer.
<!-- Pears p.298; Runciman leaf 146 (eclipse); B#18 (Wikipedia: fog 26 May, Hunyadi hope, volcanic ash — "some suggest") -->

L33
Inside the walls, the quarrels never stopped. Giustiniani had asked the grand duke, Loukas Notaras, for the cannon he controlled, to be moved to the Mesoteichion, where every gun was needed. Notaras balked. Leonard reports a bitter argument. In the end, Giustiniani seems to have got his way. But those were days, and the guns were precious, and the city had no days to spare.
<!-- Pears pp.300–303; Runciman leaf 158 -->

L33b
Even on the eve of the final assault, the city could not stop being a city. Barbaro describes Greek porters who were supposed to carry the wooden screens used to shield the defenders, and who stopped to demand their pay first. You can read that as greed. You can also read it as a population that had been promised things by its government for a very long time, and had stopped believing in them. Both readings may be true. Sieges are rarely noble in the details.
<!-- Pears p.303 (Barbaro: porters demanding payment for the wooden "mantles") — interpretation flagged as ours -->

[ON SCREEN: cảnh khắc: sultan cưỡi ngựa giữa hàng quân; nhãn "27 MAY"; mảnh tường rào đổ]
L34
On Sunday, the twenty-seventh of May, the sultan rode through his whole army to announce that the great assault would come within two days, and, we're told, that his men would have three days of plunder. The bombardment turned on the stockade across the Mesoteichion. Three direct hits from the great cannon brought a section of it down. Giustiniani was supervising the repairs when a splinter wounded him. It was slight. He had it dressed, and was back at his post before nightfall.
<!-- Runciman leaf 151; Wikipedia (three days of plunder — "reportedly") -->

L35
That was the Sunday. On Monday, the twenty-eighth, the guns went quiet. The Ottoman camp prepared. The city prayed. And a little after midnight, the drums began.

=== PART 3 ===

## Chapter 7 — The night of the twenty-ninth

[ON SCREEN: hình thành phố ban đêm; ánh đuốc; chữ "28–29 MAY 1453"; hình khắc cổng trong tường đóng lại]
L36
Let's go back to that evening. The emperor, as we saw, had gone to Hagia Sophia. He had never been crowned in that church. Tonight, he may have been saying goodbye to it. Then came the order that sealed the defenders in. As Giustiniani and his Greek and Italian comrades passed through the inner wall to take their places at the outer wall and the stockade, the gates of the inner wall were shut behind them. There was to be no retreat. Remember that detail too.
<!-- Runciman leaf 159 -->

L37
The assault began between one and two hours after midnight. First came the irregulars, the bashi-bazouks, wave after wave, meant to tire the defenders and cheap to lose. Then the troops from Anatolia. Both were thrown back. For about an hour, even the Janissaries, the sultan's own household infantry, could make no headway. Which brings us to the suspects. Let's take them one at a time.
<!-- Pears pp.334–340; Runciman leaf 163, 165 -->

[ON SCREEN: FIG. I — khẩu pháo khắc; chữ "SUSPECT ONE · THE CANNON"]
L38
Suspect number one: the cannon. The case against it is simple. For six weeks, the guns had hammered the same stretch of wall. They breached it in three places, and on the Sunday before the final night, three direct hits from the great gun brought down part of the stockade. The case for the defense is just as simple. Every night, the stockade went back up, and when the assault came, it was still there. The cannon opened the door. It did not walk through it.
<!-- Pears pp.307–309 (three breaches); Runciman leaf 151 -->

[ON SCREEN: FIG. II — cửa nhỏ khắc; chữ "SUSPECT TWO · THE KERKOPORTA"]
L39
Suspect number two: a small door. The Kerkoporta was a little sally port, half hidden by a tower, where an old wall met the great Theodosian wall. The story you'll find in many books is that somebody left it open, and Turkish soldiers wandered in. That comes mainly from one chronicler, Ducas. Critobulus, who wrote for the sultan, doesn't mention it at all. Excavations haven't found a matching gate. Which makes you wonder. But the Italian brothers called Bocchiardi were using the little door that very night, to strike back at the Turkish troops. Runciman, writing in nineteen sixty-five, doesn't blame a careless guard. In his telling, the door was open because defenders were using it.
<!-- B#23 (D); Wikipedia Kerkoporta (excavations); Pears pp.342–345; Runciman leaf 165–166 -->

[ON SCREEN: FIG. III — chân dung khắc người lính mặc giáp (không dùng mặt thật); chữ "SUSPECT THREE · GIUSTINIANI"]
L40
Suspect number three: Giustiniani. Just before sunrise, a shot fired at close range struck him and pierced his breastplate. Runciman says his nerve broke, and he demanded to be taken away. The emperor himself begged him to stay. A gate was opened, and his bodyguard carried him through the streets to a Genoese ship in the harbor. Before the gate could be shut again, the Genoese around him came streaming through after him. Barbaro, the Venetian, claimed the wound was nothing and that Giustiniani faked it out of fear. But the wound was real. He died of it on Chios a day or two later. Whether his nerve failed is another question, and his own people answered it much more kindly than the Venetians did.
<!-- Runciman leaf 165, 177; Pears pp.345–347; B#20–22 -->

[BEAT 1.4 s]
[ON SCREEN: ba hình khắc nối bằng mũi tên: pháo → người bị thương → cửa; nhãn "ONE CHAIN"]
L41
And then, suddenly, someone looked up and saw Turkish flags flying from the tower above the Kerkoporta. The cry went up: the city is taken. Do you see it? These aren't three suspects. They're three links. The cannon cracked the stockade. The bullet found Giustiniani. The fear, and the open gate, did the rest. Take away any one of them and the night might have gone differently. Or it might not. Runciman himself says that even if Giustiniani hadn't been wounded and the little door hadn't been left ajar, little would have changed in the long run.
<!-- Runciman leaf 166, 215 -->

[ON SCREEN: khắc bốn người xuống ngựa giữ lối; tên ghi nhỏ: "THEOPHILUS PALAIOLOGOS · DON FRANCISCO OF TOLEDO · JOHN DALMATA · THE EMPEROR"]
L42
The emperor was told that the Turks were inside. According to Critobulus, he cried out that the city was taken and he was still alive. Then he dismounted, and with three companions, his cousin Theophilus Palaiologos, a Spaniard called Don Francisco of Toledo, and a soldier named John Dalmata, he held the approach to the gate for a few minutes. They tried to rally the Greeks. The Greeks were beyond rallying. The gate was jammed with men trying to get out, and more and more Janissaries were falling on them. Theophilus shouted that he would rather die than live, and disappeared into the oncoming hordes.

[BEAT 1.6 s]
L43
And that's the last we see of Constantine Palaiologos. Nobody who wrote about it actually saw how he died. Critobulus says he fell fighting. Leonard says a Janissary struck him down. Ducas says his head was recognized and displayed. Phrantzes says a body wearing imperial eagles was found and given to Christians to bury. Barbaro says some claimed to have seen him among the dead, and others that he was never found. Pears judged that none of these accounts is quite trustworthy, and no one knows where he lies. Later, a legend grew that an angel turned him to marble and hid him under the Golden Gate, to wait. That one has very little support in the early sources. Which has not stopped anyone from telling it.

## Chapter 8 — What came after

[ON SCREEN: ảnh/khắc cảnh cướp phá — dùng tư liệu thật; hết sức kiềm chế, không máu me; nhãn "29 MAY 1453 · MORNING"]
L44
By about five or six in the morning, the city was in Turkish hands. The killing went on for hours, until about midday, by the account of Critobulus. Then the soldiers worked out that prisoners and treasure were worth more than corpses. Critobulus counts about four thousand dead. More than fifty thousand people, by his count, were taken as slaves. Leonard of Chios says sixty thousand. Whichever number you trust, it was the end of a world for the people in it.
<!-- Pears pp.358–364, 368–369; Runciman leaf 172 -->

[ON SCREEN: Zonaro "Mehmed II entering Constantinople" hoặc tư liệu PD tương đương; Hagia Sophia nội thất khắc]
L45
The sultan had come at dawn to look at the breach, with the waning moon still high in the sky. Then he waited until afternoon, when the first excesses of massacre and pillage were over, to make his own entry. He rode to Hagia Sophia. A soldier was prying up a piece of the marble floor. Mehmed struck him. The loot and the captives, he said, were the army's. The buildings were his. An imam climbed into the pulpit, and the Great Church became a mosque.
<!-- Runciman leaf 170; Pears pp.372–374 -->

L46
And then came the irony that Gennadius, the monk who had warned that the city would fall, was named patriarch by the sultan himself. "Be patriarch, and be at peace," Mehmed is reported to have said. The sultan took the title Caesar of Rome, on the grounds that whoever held Constantinople held the Roman Empire. He moved his capital there. Many historians use that year as the end of the Middle Ages, though like most such dates, it's a convenience. What's less of a convenience is that the age of the great wall was over. A big enough gun, and the money to build it, could do what a thousand years of armies could not.
<!-- Pears pp.381–384; Wikipedia Mehmed II (Qayser-i Rûm, Gennadius, capital); B#33 -->

L46b
There were stranger endings still. Constantine's own nephews were taken into the sultan's household. One of them, renamed Mesih Pasha, rose to be admiral of the Ottoman fleet and went on to serve twice as grand vizier. Cardinal Isidore, the Pope's man, who had come to proclaim the union in Hagia Sophia, was captured too. He was reportedly ransomed for a few small coins. The empire's last defenders died, fled, or changed sides. And the city itself became, within a generation, one of the great capitals of a new empire.

Runciman lists the ifs, and he lists them kindly. A less determined sultan. A more persuasive vizier. A Venetian fleet that sailed a fortnight earlier. Change any one, and Byzantium might have lingered on for another decade. Not much longer. So which suspect do I arrest? None of them. Or all three, which in a courtroom comes to the same thing. A twenty-one-year-old with a gun he'd bought from the other side. A wall that had held for a thousand years, held by fewer than seven thousand people. A wounded man, a door, and a few minutes of fear. That's the part I keep coming back to. The walls didn't fall because they were weak. They fell because, for one hour, at dawn, on one patch of ground, the people holding them ran out of the only thing a wall can't supply: more people.

Go back to the evening of the twenty-eighth. The emperor rides to the Great Church. He prays. By dawn, it will all be over. And somewhere, the legend says, the last Roman emperor sleeps on, in marble, under a gate in a wall that no longer stands guard. The truth is quieter. Nobody knows where Constantine lies. All we know is that on the morning of the twenty-ninth of May, the waning moon was still high in the sky when the sultan came to look at the breach.
